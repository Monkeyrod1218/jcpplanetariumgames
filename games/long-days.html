☆☆☆☆☆

For teachers and families

The big idea: Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5°. The hemisphere tilted toward the Sun gets more hours of daylight (long days) and is having summer; the hemisphere tilted away gets fewer hours of daylight (short days) and is having winter. In the data, Sydney (Southern Hemisphere) has about 14 hours of daylight, so it is summer there; Chicago (Northern Hemisphere) has about 9 hours, so it is winter there.

The evidence in the table: an early sunrise (5:47 a.m.) plus a late sunset (8:09 p.m.) adds up to a long day. That long day is the clue that Sydney is tilted toward the Sun.

Opposite seasons: only one hemisphere can lean toward the Sun at a time, so the north and south always have opposite seasons. Sydney's summer is Chicago's winter.

One simplification: the model treats tilt as the cause and keeps things symmetric. Real day lengths also depend on latitude, and these readings are near a solstice, when the difference is largest.
